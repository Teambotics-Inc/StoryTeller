# Research log: The Rosetta Stone

## Assumptions (not asked of the user)
- Subject: the object itself (the granodiorite stele fragment, British Museum EA 24) plus what made it matter: the 196 BC decree it carries, how the script was lost, how it was found and taken in 1799-1802, how it was read (1802-1824) and why it is argued over today. Not: the full history of Egyptology, the Rosetta language-software brand, or the ESA Rosetta spacecraft.
- Angle: "A boring priestly tax-and-honours decree became the key to a lost writing system because it said the same thing three times, and its fame now fuels an argument over who it belongs to."
- Audience: curious general readers. About 25-35 nodes (user's request).
- Kind: object / history. Non-brand palette chosen deliberately (see theme.json brandSources).
- Credit/disclaimer: anonymous "An independent StoryTeller study." (user gave no name).
- All sources read 2026-10-01. The British Museum and Britannica pages sit behind a bot wall for plain fetchers ("Just a moment..."), so I read them in a browser tool, from the rendered page text. Wikipedia, The National, ABC, Al Jazeera, ARCE and the other pages were fetched as plain text; the Champollion translation PDF was converted to text and quotes re-checked against it.
- Seams: no direct access to the 196 BC inscription itself (I did not read a translation of the decree text; its contents are as the British Museum summarises them). No Gazette/archival primary text for the 1799 find or the 1801 capitulation; those come from the British Museum, Britannica and Wikipedia (tertiary, flagged).

### bm-obj: British Museum collection record, "stela" EA24 (The Rosetta Stone)
- https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA24 · British Museum · accessed 2026-10-01 · tier 1 (holding institution)
- "Part of grey and pink granodiorite stela bearing priestly decree concerning Ptolemy V in three blocks of text: Hieroglyphic (14 lines), Demotic (32 lines) and Greek (54 lines)." (copied)
- Production date 196BC; Findspot "Fort Saint Julien" at Rashid; excavated by "Pierre Francois Xavier Bouchard"; Length 112.30 cm (max), Width 75.70 cm, Thickness 28.40 cm; Materials granodiorite (copied)
- "The inscription is a decree passed by a council of priests, one of a series that affirm the royal cult of the 13 year-old Ptolemy V on the first anniversary of his coronation." (copied)

### bm-blog: "The Rosetta Stone: everything you need to know"
- https://www.britishmuseum.org/blog/everything-you-ever-wanted-know-about-rosetta-stone · British Museum blog (published 14 July 2017, last updated 2 July 2026) · accessed 2026-10-01 · tier 1
- "The inscriptions on the Rosetta Stone were carved in 196 BC"; Ptolemy V "(reign 204–181 BC)" (copied)
- "To date, 29 of these decrees on stones have been discovered across Egypt, starting with the Rosetta Stone in 1799. The majority of them (22) remain in Egypt." (copied)
- "the story most generally accepted is that it was found by accident by soldiers on 15 July 1799 while digging the foundations of an addition to a fort near Rashid (Rosetta)." and "accounts of the Stone's discovery in July 1799 are now rather vague" (copied)
- "The officer in charge, Pierre-François Bouchard (1771–1822), realised the importance of the discovery." (copied)
- "the text on the Stone is a partial copy of a prototype that was composed about a century earlier in the 3rd century BC" (copied)
- Decree "recounts real events from the reign of Ptolemy V, including the turmoil of the Great Rebellion, a sweeping Egyptian uprising that challenged Ptolemaic rule for nearly two decades (204–186 BC)." (copied)
- "Thomas Young (1773–1829), an English physicist, was one of the first to show that some of the hieroglyphs on the Rosetta Stone wrote the sounds of a royal name, that of Ptolemy." (copied)
- Champollion announced at the Academie des Inscriptions et Belles Lettres "on Friday 27 September 1822"; audience included Young (copied, paraphrased)
- Champollion's second step: alphabetic signs used also for Egyptian names; "Together with his knowledge of the Coptic language" this let him begin reading inscriptions fully (paraphrased)
- "On Napoleon's defeat, the Stone became the property of the British under the terms of the Treaty of Alexandria (1801)"; arrived Portsmouth February 1802; "presented to the British Museum by George III in July of that year." (copied)
- On display since 1802 "with only one break": 1917, "two years in a station on the Postal Tube Railway 50 feet below the ground at Holborn." (copied)
- Note: BM text on this page says the stela was a decree "on the first anniversary of his coronation (in 196 BC)".

### bm-timeline: "How Egyptian hieroglyphs were decoded, a timeline to decipherment"
- https://www.britishmuseum.org/exhibitions/hieroglyphs-unlocking-ancient-egypt/egyptian-hieroglyphs-decipherment-timeline · British Museum · accessed 2026-10-01 · tier 1

- AD 900s: medieval Arab travellers/scholars tried to decipher hieroglyphs (paraphrased)
- 1400-1700: "European scholars still believed that hieroglyphs represented concepts as symbols, rather than a written language." (copied)
- "Napoleon Bonaparte invaded Egypt in 1798, bringing with him 40,000 troops"; "He unusually also brought a large team of scholars and scientists". (copied)
- "Prints and casts of the Rosetta Stone were distributed across Europe" (copied)
- "On 14 September 1822, Champollion excitedly visited his brother. Waving his notes in the air, he gasped, 'Je tiens l'affaire, vois!' (Look, I've got it!) before promptly collapsing." (copied)
- "The public reading of this letter on 27 September 1822 is considered the moment of decipherment." (copied)

### wiki: Wikipedia, "Rosetta Stone"
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosetta_Stone · Wikipedia · accessed 2026-10-01 · tier 3 (orientation; used only where noted and corroborated by tier 1/2 or flagged)
- Granodiorite; "These additions were removed when the stone was cleaned in 1999" (chalk and carnauba wax had darkened it and caused the "mistaken identification as black basalt") (copied)
- Found "mid-July 1799" by soldiers of Colonel d'Hautpoul at Fort Julien; Lancret's report "received at a meeting of the Institute on 19 July" (paraphrased)
- Conté "found a way to use the stone itself as a printing block"; prints taken to Paris by General Dugua; 1802 Society of Antiquaries made four plaster casts (paraphrased)
- Menou surrendered 30 August 1801; "contemporary accounts differ" on how the stone passed into British hands; Wikipedia names it the Capitulation of Alexandria (BM says Treaty of Alexandria)
- First published Greek translations 1803 (Ameilhon; Heyne); Weston English translation presented April 1802
- 1802: Silvestre de Sacy identified five names in the Demotic; Åkerblad "published an alphabet of 29 letters (more than half of which were correct)" (copied)
- 1814: Young found p-t-o-l-m-e-s in the hieroglyphic text and "as many as 80 similarities" with the Demotic (paraphrased)
- Champollion completed the alphabet 14 Sept 1822, announced 27 Sept 1822; letter to Dacier same day; confirmed 1823 with Ramesses and Thutmose at Abu Simbel
- 1823: James Browne, anonymously in Edinburgh Review, alleged the "unscrupulous" Champollion plagiarised Young (copied word)
- Zahi Hawass called for return in July 2003; "icon of our Egyptian identity" (copied); 2005 BM gave Egypt a replica
- "According to the Oxford English Dictionary, the first figurative use of the term appeared in the 1902 edition of the Encyclopædia Britannica" (copied; OED not independently checked)
- Horapollo's 5th-century Hieroglyphica was "misleading in many ways" (copied) -- used only inside a `details` hedge

## Conflicts and gaps
- Find date: BM 15 July 1799 (itself hedged as "most generally accepted"); Wikipedia and ARCE "July 1799"; The National (2026) says 15 July is commonly accepted but Egypt's antiquities ministry marks 19 July; Britannica "August 1799". Modelled as its own node; unresolved.
- Material: BM, ARCE, Carnegie, The National (2026) say granodiorite; Britannica says "black granite" (and captions "basalt slab"); Merriam-Webster "black basalt"; ABC and Al Jazeera "dark grey granite". Wikipedia says wax/chalk darkening caused the basalt label (the BM conservation text mentions wax but not chalk or basalt). Modelled as a node; each source attributed, not resolved.
- Ptolemy V reign: BM and Carnegie 204-181 BC/BCE; Britannica 205-180 BCE. BM figures used (node start/end unchanged); Britannica noted in details.
- Treaty name: BM, ARCE, Al Jazeera, The National (2026) "Treaty of Alexandria"; ABC "1801 surrender deal between the generals of the two sides"; Wikipedia "Capitulation of Alexandria"; The National (2022) says "a treaty in 1802" (reported as a discrepancy). Who physically handed the stone over is not stated in the independent sources read; only Wikipedia says accounts differ.
- Credit for decipherment: Britannica says "largely the work of Thomas Young ... and ... Champollion"; BM says Champollion's 1822 reading "is considered the moment of decipherment"; rivalry modelled as its own node.
- Coronation vs anniversary: BM says the decree marks the first anniversary of the coronation; ARCE says the synod celebrated the coronation of the previous day; Britannica says the ninth year of his reign, commemorating accession. Shown side by side in the decree node.
- Not covered: the content of the decree beyond BM's summary; Salvolini plagiarism episode; Rosetta software/spacecraft.
