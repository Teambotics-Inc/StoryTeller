# Research log: the word "salary"

## Brief
- Subject: the English word *salary* (kind: word/etymology)
- Angle: A word for pay that was built on salt, and the honest answer to "were Roman soldiers paid in salt?" is "probably not, and nobody is sure what the salt was".
- Audience: curious general readers. Boundaries: Latin to modern English, plus Japan's borrowing; not a history of wages in general.
- Assumptions (no questions asked): independent study, light archive theme.

## Sources (all accessed 2026-09-30)

### ety: Online Etymology Dictionary, "salary"
- https://www.etymonline.com/word/salary · Etymonline · tier 3 (reference, cites scholars)
- Noun, late 13c.: from Anglo-French salarie, Old French salaire, from Latin salarium "an allowance, a stipend, a pension". Scholars suggest it was "salt-money, soldier's allowance for the purchase of salt".
- Verb, late 15c., in Caxton: "to pay a regular salary to". Adjective salaried c. 1600, first opposed to honorary, later to hourly.
- By the 19th century narrowed to "recompense stipulated to be paid a person periodically for services". Comparable Greek term sitērion "pay", from sitos "wheat, corn".
- Via Salaria named because Sabines used it to fetch sea-salt. Japanese sarariman borrowed from English.

### wikt-en: Wiktionary, "salary"
- https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/salary · tier 3
- Salarium is the neuter of adjective salarius "related to salt", from sal.
- "It is generally assumed that salārium was an abbreviation of salārium argentum ('salt money'), though that phrase is not attested." An alternative: "money used to buy salt [and other miscellaneous items]". The soldiers-paid-in-salt theory lacks ancient documentation.

### wikt-la: Wiktionary, "salarium" (Latin)
- https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/salarium · tier 3
- Senses: "a salary, stipend, allowance, pension"; "a meal". Nominalization of salarius; via Proto-Italic to PIE. Descendants: Italian salario, Spanish salario, French salaire, Dutch salaris, English salary.

### pie: Wiktionary, PIE root for "salt"
- https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Reconstruction:Proto-Indo-European/seh%E2%82%82l- · tier 3 (reconstruction)
- Root *sḗh₂l "salt". Descendants listed: Latin sal, Greek hā́ls, Proto-Germanic *saltą (English salt), Proto-Slavic/Old Church Slavonic solь, Latvian sāls.

### wiki-sal: Wikipedia, "Salary"
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salary · tier 3
- "The word 'salary' comes from Latin salarium 'pertaining to salt' (sal 'salt'), but the exact connection is not known."
- "a persistent modern claim that the Roman Legions were sometimes paid in salt is baseless." "It may have been a salt allowance, but even that is not supported by ancient sources."
- "being worth one's salt" is said to descend from the salarium.
- "A cuneiform inscribed clay tablet dated about 3100 BCE provides a record of the daily beer rations for workers in Mesopotamia."
- Medieval salaried work "relatively rare and mostly limited to servants and higher status roles"; pay often lodging, food, livery clothes.
- Second Industrial Revolution (1870-1930) brought salaried managers; office work "hard to measure hourly or piecewise". Japan coined "salaryman". Salary vs hourly wage first codified by the US Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938.

### wiki-via: Wikipedia, "Via Salaria"
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Via_Salaria · tier 3
- "The Via Salaria owes its name to the Latin word for 'salt'", route by which Sabines fetched salt from the marshes at the mouth of the Tiber; peoples nearer the Adriatic used it for Adriatic salt.

### wiki-hs: Wikipedia, "History of salt"
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_salt · tier 3
- Roads built in the early Roman Republic to bring salt to Rome. "a salārium may have been an allowance paid to Roman soldiers for the purchase of salt, but even that is not well established."

### etymologia: etymologia.org, "Were Roman Soldiers Really Paid in Salt?"
- https://etymologia.org/word-myths/salary-salt-roman-soldiers-myth/ · tier 3-4 (hobbyist site; used only for the disagreement)
- Quotes Pliny (Natural History) as: "In the old days, it was with salt that the valor of soldiers was rewarded, whence the Latin word for pay is derived."
- "Historians have found no Roman military payroll records that specify payment in salt rather than coin." Suggests an allowance to buy salt.

### cover: James Coverley, "Were Roman Soldiers Paid in Salt?"
- https://jamescoverley.substack.com/p/were-roman-soldiers-paid-in-salt · tier 3-4 (Substack essay; used only for the disagreement)
- Renders Pliny NH 31.89 as: "[salt] is also related to magistracies and duty abroad, and that's where we get the word 'salaries'", and argues Pliny is discussing salt taxation, not soldiers' pay. Cites Livy 29.37.3 on a salt tax. CONFLICTS with etymologia's rendering of the same author.

### wage: Etymonline, "wage"
- https://www.etymonline.com/word/wage · tier 3
- c. 1300 "a payment for services rendered, reward, just deserts"; mid-14c. "salary paid to a provider of service"; also "a pledge, guarantee, surety". From Anglo-French/Old North French wage, Frankish *wadja-.

### stipend: Etymonline, "stipend"
- https://www.etymonline.com/word/stipend · tier 3
- Early 15c. From Latin stipendium "tax, impost, tribute," in military use "pay, salary", from stips "small payment" + pendere "to hang; weigh; pay".

### salaryman: Wikipedia, "Salaryman"
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salaryman · tier 3
- Derived from English "salaried man" (sararīman). Widely used in the Taishō and early Shōwa periods; after WWII "a symbol of the typical Japanese worker".

### flsa: US Dept of Labor, Fair Labor Standards Act
- https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/flsa · tier 1
- Sets standards for "minimum wage, overtime pay, recordkeeping, and youth employment". Page refers to salary-based exemptions for executive, administrative and professional staff. (Did not state the 1938 enactment date; that comes from wiki-sal.)

## Gaps and conflicts
- Core conflict: how salarium got from "salt" to "pay". Options: salt allowance; "salt money" (phrase unattested); discretionary money (Coverley's view). Also the two secondary renderings of Pliny disagree.
- Not covered: OED first citation dates, Pliny's Latin read first-hand, Roman payroll detail, non-European equivalents.
